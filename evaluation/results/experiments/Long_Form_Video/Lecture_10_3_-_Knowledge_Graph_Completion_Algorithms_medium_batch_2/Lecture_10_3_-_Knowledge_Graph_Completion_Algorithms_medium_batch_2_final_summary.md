# Final Multimodal Video Summary

The video introduces the task of Knowledge Graph Completion (KGC), which involves predicting missing information within a knowledge graph by identifying the tail entity given a head node and a relation type. This task is distinguished from classical link prediction by specifying the head node. The fundamental approach relies on assigning shallow embeddings to entities and learning relation embeddings such that the embedding of the head and relation approximates the embedding of the tail.

TransE is presented as a foundational model for this, learning embeddings for a triple $(h, r, t)$ where the sum of the head embedding and the relation embedding approximates the tail embedding ($h + r \approx t$). This allows for translation or inference, enabling a model to move from an entity (head), apply a relation vector, and land on the target entity (tail). While TransE can model inverse relations and transitive relationships by learning relation-specific vectors, its limitations become apparent when attempting to model complex relational patterns. Specifically, standard TransE struggles to model symmetric relationships and one-to-N relations.

To address these limitations, ColdTransR, which utilizes TransAR, is introduced. This method employs a relation-specific projection matrix and a transformation matrix to move entity embeddings into a specialized space, allowing the system to handle symmetric relations and model distinct entities without collision. This advancement enables the modeling of both symmetric and antisymmetric relations.

Further exploring different modeling techniques, the video discusses the linear modeling approach of SRM. Unlike previous distance-based scoring functions, SRM learns entities and relations as vectors in an embedding space, utilizing a bilinear scoring function. This function calculates the score by multiplying the coordinates of the head, relation, and tail, effectively defining a hyperplane in the embedding space. While this model can handle symmetric relations and one-to-N relations, it cannot model composition relations.

The discussion then shifts to complex vector embeddings, which expand the model space to include real and imaginary parts. These complex embeddings provide greater expressivity, allowing the system to model symmetric, inverse, and antisymmetric relations. However, these complex approaches face limitations, as they still cannot model composition or one-to-many relations due to the nature of their scoring functions.

In conclusion, the video reviews the diversity of knowledge graph embedding methods, including TransE, TransR, DistMult, and complex models. The optimal choice of method depends heavily on the desired relationship types. For instance, while simple methods like TransE are useful, advanced models like TransZ, which use complex embeddings, are necessary if composite or complex relations are desired. Ultimately, the selection of an embedding method should align with the specific relational patterns and prediction goals of the knowledge graph.
