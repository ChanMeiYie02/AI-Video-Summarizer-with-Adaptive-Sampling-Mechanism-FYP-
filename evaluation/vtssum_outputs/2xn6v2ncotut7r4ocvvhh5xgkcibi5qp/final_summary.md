The presentation begins by introducing Gaussian Processes (GPs) and outlining a philosophy that combines these probabilistic methods with mechanistic models to study transcriptional regulation, emphasizing the broad implications for machine learning and pattern recognition. The speaker sets the stage by discussing the necessity of applying systems biology principles to other domains, arguing that biological systems are inherently robust and require quantitative, predictive models.

The core methodology proposed involves bridging the gap between purely data-driven analysis and purely mechanistic modeling. Systems biologists combine these approaches by using computational models to identify and parameterize functional modules within biological data. While initial approaches include clustering to hypothesize co-regulated genes, a more detailed method involves creating a full mechanistic model and fitting kinetic parameters to the data. The speaker highlights the challenges of mechanistic modeling, noting that it is highly demanding for large datasets due to the need to account for every cellular process and the difficulty of fitting all necessary parameters.

The discussion traces the historical development of modeling, contrasting traditional mechanistic models with Gauss’s approach of combining mechanistic models with data fitting. This leads to the proposal that computational models should serve as a necessary transition path toward more complex methods, such as stochastic differential equations and Gaussian Processes.

A key example is the P53 protein pathway, which is modeled to understand its network structure. This involves time-series analysis of gene expression following P53 activation, using clustering to identify potential targets, and modeling the transcriptional rates. The goal is to estimate the activity of transcription factors by fitting differential equation models to gene expression data and confirming these results with experimental validation.

The central focus then shifts to the application of Gaussian Processes (GPs). The speaker details how GPs are used to handle missing variables probabilistically, defining the multivariate Gaussian distribution and analyzing the resulting correlation structure between variables. Specific analysis techniques, including examining conditional distributions and deriving covariance functions, are used to understand the relationship between observed data and the underlying unobserved function.

The framework utilizes a specific exponentiated quadratic covariance function, which is combined with a bias term to create a robust function capable of incorporating noise and allowing the function to move, thereby addressing limitations in simpler models. This structure is integrated with an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process to define a unique stationary Gaussian Markov process.

The overall methodology involves nonparametric modeling, where a covariance function is combined with observations to predict the unobserved function. The process involves carefully sampling data and defining constraints to ensure the predicted function is tightly constrained near the observed data, enhancing confidence through rigidity.
