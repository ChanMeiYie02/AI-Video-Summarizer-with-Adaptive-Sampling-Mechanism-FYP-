# Final Multimodal Video Summary

This research is undertaken by Peng Run, Butt, and Tatiana Alexi, in collaboration with Rich Wilson, and is supported by the Royal Society and Europe. The primary goal of this work is to address the challenge of efficiently characterizing variations in graph structure arising from different data sources, seeking methods that avoid exhaustive matching.

The discussion begins by introducing various approaches, including models based on random walks and fusion processes on graphs. Characterization methods range from simple topological measures (such as average degree and edge density) to more sophisticated techniques utilizing graph spectral methods and algebraic methods. A key focus is placed on using the heat kernel trace to distinguish graph structures. The research explores how moments of the heat kernel trace can be linked to Zeta functions, enabling the distinction between different graph types.

The work further introduces the Norouzi function and the Harris Teeter function, which are designed to capture topological, spectral, and algebraic properties of a graph. Specifically, the function is derived by transforming the graph into an orientated line graph, and its coefficients are linked to symmetric polynomials of eigenvalues. This approach, utilizing the characteristic polynomial of the orientated line graph's adjacency matrix, allows for graph pattern analysis. Furthermore, a novel representation, the Harazi function, is explored, linking the graph structure to the spectrum of discrete-time quantum walks and symmetric polynomials of eigenvalues. Derivatives of this function are used to compute cycle frequencies, providing a method to project out prime cycles without extensive searching.

Experimental results demonstrate that using higher-order coefficients provides the clearest separation and best classification results when distinguishing graph structures across various representations. This efficacy is particularly noted when analyzing the normalized Laplacian for datasets like the Penguins dataset.

The research then extends these concepts to hypergraphs, addressing the difficulty of distinguishing between graphs (pairwise relationships) and hypergraphs (higher-order relationships). A method is proposed involving the O'Hara representation—converting a hypergraph into a standard graph format via a sequence of transformations—to generate a representation based on the characteristic polynomials. This representation successfully distinguishes hypergraphs, and the technique is validated using real-world datasets, including ModelHouse image sequences and cloud data, showing effective separation using higher coefficients.

Finally, the segment investigates the relationship between different representations of the quantum walk support matrix to detect local graph symmetries. The goal is to compare representations based on the positive support of the matrix versus the positive support of its third power. The discussion also addresses how graphs are perturbed and noisy, involving methods to model changes through node and edge modifications. The research also notes the practical challenges in reconstructing original graph matrices from their spectrum, highlighting the difficulties involved in recovering eigenvalues and eigenvectors, which are crucial for applying these spectral methods.
